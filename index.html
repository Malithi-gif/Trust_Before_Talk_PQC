Keys, parameters and what each request carries

Public keys are provisioned in advance and trusted. The sender seals each request to the receiver's ML-KEM public key and signs the whole envelope with its own ML-DSA key. The keys below were generated in your browser when the page loaded.

Signing the tier is essential. Anyone can encapsulate to a receiver's public key, so without a signature an attacker could forge a LOW request or lower the tier of a real one.

Observer lab: the protection reveals the risk

The observer sits outside TLS, behind one gateway address, and records only the length of each request. It trains a k-nearest-neighbour classifier (k = 5) on half the workflows and guesses the tier of every request in the other half. Mixed workflows have 3 to 12 requests with two actions per tier.

Costs, mitigations and lessons

Adaptation saves 1,606 bytes on each LOW request but adds 2,406 bytes on each CRITICAL one, so it only pays off with more than 1.5 LOW requests per CRITICAL request. Every padding scheme that removes the leak costs more than that.

Paper: Intel i7-10700K, Python pqcrypto. Here: your browser running JavaScript. ML-DSA signing dominates sender time (about 99% in the paper).

Lesson 1

Make policy deterministic

Minimum protection and authorization come from the action, not the model. Risk assessment may raise protection but never lower it.

Lesson 2

Sizes are a side channel

Risk-dependent artifacts such as tokens, labels and parameter sets announce themselves through their length, even inside TLS.

Lesson 3

Prefer uniform parameters

Choose one parameter set from confidentiality and signature-lifetime needs, and carry authorization as a fixed-size reference.
